Typhoid Fever — Recognition and Emergency Action in Disaster Settings

Typhoid fever is a severe bacterial infection caused by Salmonella Typhi. It is transmitted through food or water contaminated with human faeces. Typhoid is one of the most common infections in Pakistan following floods, when sewage systems are damaged and drinking water is contaminated. Large outbreaks occurred after the 2010 and 2022 Pakistan floods, affecting hundreds of thousands.

SYMPTOMS

Typhoid develops 1–3 weeks after infection. It does not develop overnight. Early recognition is important because untreated typhoid can be fatal.

Week 1: Gradually rising fever (can reach 39–40°C / 102–104°F), headache, generalised body aches, fatigue, loss of appetite, and sometimes a dry cough.

Week 2: Sustained high fever, slow heart rate despite high temperature (a distinctive feature), abdominal pain and bloating, constipation or diarrhoea, and a rash of flat rose-coloured spots on the trunk (seen in some patients).

Week 3 onward (if untreated): Risk of life-threatening complications including intestinal perforation (sudden severe abdominal pain and vomiting), internal bleeding, sepsis, and confusion.

WHEN TO SEEK EMERGENCY CARE IMMEDIATELY

Go to the nearest hospital without delay if the patient develops:
Sudden severe abdominal pain or rigidity of the abdomen — this may indicate intestinal perforation, a surgical emergency.
Vomiting blood or passing blood in stools.
High fever with confusion, delirium, or loss of consciousness.
Severe weakness and inability to eat or drink — risk of collapse.
Seizures.

IMMEDIATE ACTIONS BEFORE REACHING HOSPITAL

Keep the patient lying down and resting.
Encourage fluid intake (oral rehydration salts / ORS, clean boiled water, or clear fluids) if conscious and not vomiting — typhoid causes significant fluid loss.
Reduce fever with paracetamol. Do not use aspirin or ibuprofen — these can increase risk of bleeding.
Do not give antidiarrhoeals (like loperamide) — these trap bacteria in the gut.

TREATMENT

Typhoid is treated with antibiotics. A doctor will prescribe the appropriate antibiotic based on local resistance patterns. In Pakistan, fluoroquinolone resistance is common; azithromycin or ceftriaxone may be prescribed. Severely ill patients require hospital admission and intravenous antibiotics.

Do not self-medicate with antibiotics — the wrong antibiotic can mask symptoms and lead to delayed diagnosis or resistance.

PREVENTION IN FLOOD-AFFECTED AREAS

Drink only boiled or bottled water with unbroken seals.
Wash hands with soap and water before eating and after using the toilet.
Avoid eating raw vegetables, salads, and unpeeled fruit from street vendors.
Avoid ice in drinks unless you know it is made from safe water.
Typhoid vaccination is available and recommended for individuals in endemic and flood-affected areas.
Report suspected typhoid cases to the nearest relief medical team — rapid containment prevents outbreak spread.

TYPHOID IN CHILDREN

Children under 5 in Pakistan are at highest risk. A child with sustained high fever lasting more than 3 days without an obvious cause should be tested for typhoid. Do not wait for the rash or abdominal symptoms — seek care early.
