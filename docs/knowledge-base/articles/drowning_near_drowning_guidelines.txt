Drowning and Near-Drowning — Emergency First Aid

A person who appears to recover after submersion can deteriorate hours later due to secondary drowning — always take any near-drowning victim to hospital even if they seem well.

RESCUING A DROWNING PERSON — RESCUER SAFETY FIRST

Do not jump into fast-moving or deep water unless you are a trained swimmer with rescue equipment. More rescuers die attempting untrained water rescues than victims.

Safe rescue techniques (in order of preference):
REACH — Extend a hand, rope, towel, branch, or clothing from the shore or boat without entering the water.
THROW — Throw a rope, life ring, empty jerrycan, or any buoyant object within reach of the victim.
WADE — If water is shallow and calm, wade toward the victim from the side, using a stick or rope for the victim to grab.
ROW — If a boat is available, row to the victim and pull them aboard.
GO — Enter the water only as a last resort, and only if you are a confident swimmer. Approach the victim from behind to avoid being grabbed and pulled under.

IMMEDIATE ACTIONS AFTER REMOVAL FROM WATER

1. Check responsiveness: call their name, tap their shoulders.
2. If unresponsive and not breathing: begin CPR immediately. Do not waste time trying to drain water from the lungs — CPR is the priority.
3. If breathing: place in the recovery position (on their side, head tilted back slightly). Monitor breathing continuously.

CPR FOR NEAR-DROWNING

Unlike standard hands-only CPR, drowning victims also require rescue breaths because the cause of cardiac arrest is lack of oxygen, not a heart rhythm problem.

Give 5 initial rescue breaths before starting chest compressions.
Then continue: 30 chest compressions followed by 2 rescue breaths.
Compress the chest hard and fast — at least 5 cm deep, 100–120 per minute.
Continue until the person breathes normally or emergency help arrives.

Rescue breathing: tilt the head back, lift the chin, pinch the nose closed, and give a full breath watching for the chest to rise.

AFTER RECOVERY — HOSPITAL IS STILL REQUIRED

Even if the victim appears to have fully recovered — walking, talking, normal breathing — they must go to hospital. Secondary drowning can occur 1–24 hours after the event. Water that entered the lungs causes delayed swelling and respiratory failure. This can kill a child or adult who walked away from the water seemingly fine.

Take any near-drowning victim to the nearest hospital regardless of how they feel. Inform the hospital that submersion occurred.

WARNING SIGNS OF SECONDARY DROWNING (1–24 hours after incident):
Increasing difficulty breathing or persistent coughing.
Extreme fatigue or persistent sleepiness.
Confusion or unusual behaviour.
Chest pain.
Bluish tinge to the lips or skin.

HYPOTHERMIA IN NEAR-DROWNING

Submersion in Pakistan's floodwaters — which are often cold — causes rapid hypothermia. After removing the victim from water:
Remove wet clothing immediately.
Wrap in blankets, including the head.
Do not rub the limbs vigorously.
Rewarm slowly in a warm environment.
Give warm sweet drinks only if the person is fully conscious and not vomiting.

PREVENTION DURING FLOODS

Never cross floodwater on foot without checking depth and speed. Moving water above knee height is dangerous for adults.
Keep children away from all flood-affected water bodies, ditches, and drains.
Do not allow children to play near waterlogged areas — open drains and pits may be invisible under murky floodwater.
If you must cross water, use a pole to check the ground ahead of each step.
