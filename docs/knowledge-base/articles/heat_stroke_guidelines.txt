Heat Exhaustion and Heatstroke — Emergency Recognition and Action

Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency in Pakistan. Extreme heat during summer monsoon floods creates a double hazard — people are simultaneously exposed to high temperatures and physically exhausted from evacuation and relief activities. Pakistan's 2015 Karachi heatwave killed over 1,200 people in days. During flood displacement, people sheltering in open areas without shade face serious heatstroke risk.

HEAT-RELATED ILLNESS: THREE STAGES

Understanding the progression helps you act early.

Stage 1 — Heat Cramps: Painful muscle cramps in the legs or abdomen after physical activity in heat. Skin is moist. Person is alert. Move to shade, give oral rehydration salts or water with a pinch of salt.

Stage 2 — Heat Exhaustion: The body is struggling to cool itself. Symptoms: heavy sweating, cool and pale or flushed skin, fast and weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, headache, dizziness, fainting, muscle cramps, extreme tiredness. The person remains conscious and coherent.

Stage 3 — Heatstroke: A medical emergency. The cooling system has failed. Symptoms: body temperature above 40°C (104°F), hot and dry skin (sweating stops), rapid and strong pulse, confusion, slurred speech, agitation, seizures, loss of consciousness. This can progress to brain damage and death within minutes.

EMERGENCY ACTIONS FOR HEATSTROKE

Call for emergency help immediately — Rescue 1122, Edhi 115.
Begin aggressive cooling at once. Every minute of delay causes further organ damage.

Cooling methods — use as many as simultaneously available:
Move the person to shade or a cool indoor area.
Remove excess clothing.
Pour cool (not ice cold) water over the entire body, especially the neck, armpits, and groin where large blood vessels run close to the skin.
Fan the person vigorously while their skin is wet — this dramatically accelerates heat loss.
Apply wet cloths or cool damp towels to the neck, armpits, groin, and wrists.
If ice is available, wrap in cloth and apply to armpits, groin, and neck (do not apply ice directly to skin).

Do not give aspirin or paracetamol for heatstroke — these fever medications do not work for heatstroke, which is not caused by infection.

If the person is conscious and not vomiting: give cool water or ORS to drink.
If the person is confused, vomiting, or unconscious: do not give anything by mouth — they may inhale it.
If the person loses consciousness: place them in the recovery position and monitor breathing. Begin CPR if they stop breathing.

Continue cooling until temperature drops and the person becomes more alert, or until emergency services arrive.

ACTIONS FOR HEAT EXHAUSTION

Move to shade or a cool area immediately.
Lie the person down and elevate their legs slightly.
Remove excess clothing and apply cool wet cloths to the face, neck, and wrists.
Give oral rehydration salts or cool water to drink slowly — about 150–200 ml every 15 minutes.
Monitor closely — if they do not improve within 30 minutes, or if they become confused, this has progressed to heatstroke. Begin aggressive cooling and call for emergency help.

CHILDREN AND HEATSTROKE

Children are more vulnerable to heatstroke than adults — they have a higher surface area to body mass ratio and cannot communicate thirst reliably.
Never leave a child in a closed vehicle, tent, or enclosed space in heat.
Infants in arms in direct sun can develop heatstroke rapidly — cover with a light cloth and move to shade.
Signs in children: excessive crying, then sudden quietness, hot flushed skin, very high fever, confusion or unusual drowsiness.

PREVENTION IN DISASTER SETTINGS

Drink water regularly — do not wait to feel thirsty. In extreme heat, adults need at least 2–3 litres of water per day.
Avoid physical activity during the hottest part of the day (11:00–15:00 hours).
Wear light, loose, light-coloured clothing.
Set up shade structures for children, elderly, and sick individuals as a priority in relief camps.
If ORS is not available, make a solution: 1 litre of clean water + 6 teaspoons of sugar + half a teaspoon of salt.

HIGH-RISK GROUPS

Prioritise monitoring for heatstroke in: elderly individuals, children under 5, pregnant women, people with heart disease or diabetes, and anyone performing heavy physical labour in heat.
