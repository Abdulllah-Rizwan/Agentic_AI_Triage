Crush Injury and Traumatic Entrapment — Emergency First Aid

Crush injuries occur when a body part is compressed under heavy weight — most commonly from building collapse, floods undermining structures, or landslides. Crush injuries are life-threatening even after the victim is freed — medical complications can develop suddenly after rescue.

CRUSH SYNDROME — THE HIDDEN DANGER

When muscle is crushed for more than one hour, toxic substances (myoglobin and potassium) are released into the bloodstream when pressure is released. This causes:
Sudden kidney failure (dark brown or tea-coloured urine is a warning sign).
Dangerously high potassium levels causing cardiac arrest.
Shock and collapse — sometimes appearing hours after rescue when the victim seemed stable.

This is called crush syndrome. A person who was trapped and then freed may appear alert but can collapse and die within hours if not hospitalized.

BEFORE FREEING A TRAPPED VICTIM

If a person has been trapped for more than one hour under heavy weight:
Alert emergency services before freeing them if possible — intravenous fluid should ideally be started before or immediately at the time of release.
Establish voice contact: reassure the person and ask them to describe their pain and sensations.
Do not give water through compressed soft tissue — give water only if there is a clear unobstructed airway.
If freeing is unavoidable without medical support, begin IV fluids immediately upon access if available.

AFTER FREEING THE VICTIM

Lay the person flat. Do not allow them to stand or walk — movement accelerates toxin release.
Check for and control any external bleeding with direct pressure.
Immobilize injured limbs — do not attempt to straighten broken bones.
Monitor breathing. Begin CPR immediately if they stop breathing.
Watch for signs of shock: pale cold skin, rapid weak pulse, confusion, or loss of consciousness.

RECOGNIZING SHOCK — ACT IMMEDIATELY

Shock after crush injury requires emergency hospital care. Signs include:
Pale, cold, clammy skin.
Rapid and weak pulse.
Rapid shallow breathing.
Confusion or drowsiness.
Loss of consciousness.

Do not give food or water to a patient in shock. Keep them lying down with legs elevated slightly (unless a head or spinal injury is suspected). Keep them warm. Get to hospital immediately.

SPINAL INJURY — DO NOT MOVE WITHOUT CARE

If a person has been in a collapsed building or buried under debris:
Assume a spinal (neck or back) injury is possible.
Do not bend, twist, or move the neck or spine.
If they must be moved due to immediate danger (fire, collapse), keep the head, neck, and back in a straight line — one person holds the head steady while others move the body as a unit.
Place on a rigid flat surface (wooden board, door) if available.

FRACTURES FROM CRUSH INJURY

Crush injuries frequently fracture multiple bones. Field immobilization:
Splint the limb in the position found using a padded rigid board, branches, or rolled clothing.
For leg fractures: tie the injured leg to the uninjured leg with cloth strips.
For arm fractures: tie the arm to a padded board and then support in a sling.
Do not try to realign a bone — immobilize it as found.
Cover open fractures (bone through skin) with a clean damp cloth. Do not push the bone back in.

COMPARTMENT SYNDROME

Severe crush or fracture can trap fluids in muscle compartments, cutting off blood supply. Symptoms: severe pain out of proportion to the injury, tight or shiny skin, extreme pain when the limb is moved or the muscle is squeezed, numbness or tingling, pale or bluish skin on the affected limb.

This is a surgical emergency. Do not delay transport to hospital.

IMMEDIATE HOSPITAL REQUIREMENT

All crush injury victims — regardless of how stable they appear — must reach hospital. Blood tests, IV fluids, and urine monitoring are required to prevent kidney failure and cardiac arrest from crush syndrome.
