Hypertensive Emergency — Recognition and Immediate Action

A hypertensive emergency is a sudden severe rise in blood pressure — typically 180/120 mmHg or higher — combined with damage to organs such as the brain, heart, or kidneys. It is a life-threatening condition requiring immediate hospital care.

In disaster settings, patients with existing high blood pressure often run out of their medications, face extreme stress, and cannot reach clinics. This significantly raises the risk of a hypertensive emergency.

RECOGNIZING A HYPERTENSIVE EMERGENCY

Seek emergency care immediately if a person with known or suspected high blood pressure develops any of the following:

Severe headache that comes on suddenly — often described as the worst headache of their life.
Chest pain or tightness — may indicate a heart attack or heart failure triggered by high pressure.
Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath — fluid may be building in the lungs.
Blurred vision, double vision, or sudden loss of vision — high pressure can damage blood vessels in the eyes and brain.
Confusion, difficulty speaking, or sudden weakness or numbness in the face, arm, or leg — these are signs of stroke.
Nausea and vomiting accompanying a severe headache.
Nosebleeds that are severe and difficult to control.
Seizures in a person with no prior history of epilepsy.
Irregular heartbeat or pounding sensation in the chest.

IMMEDIATE ACTIONS

Call for emergency help: Rescue 1122 (Punjab), Edhi Foundation 115, or transport to the nearest hospital immediately.

Keep the person calm and seated or lying down. Stress and physical exertion worsen blood pressure.

If the person has their prescribed blood pressure medication available and is conscious, encourage them to take it as directed.

Do not give medications that have not been prescribed for them.

Do not give aspirin unless specifically prescribed — aspirin treats heart attacks but is not appropriate for all hypertensive events.

Loosen tight clothing around the chest and neck.

If the person loses consciousness, check for breathing and begin CPR if they are not breathing.

Monitor and reassure the person while waiting for help. Do not leave them alone.

STROKE — RECOGNIZE FAST

A hypertensive emergency can cause a stroke. Use the FAST test:
Face — is one side of the face drooping? Ask them to smile.
Arms — can they raise both arms? Does one drift downward?
Speech — is their speech slurred or difficult to understand?
Time — if any of these signs are present, get emergency help immediately.

Every minute without treatment during a stroke causes further brain damage. Time is critical.

HEART ATTACK WARNING SIGNS

Hypertension is a major risk factor for heart attack. Warning signs include: crushing or squeezing chest pain that may spread to the left arm, jaw, or back; sweating; nausea; shortness of breath; a sense of impending doom.

Call for emergency help immediately. Keep the person still and calm. Do not give food or water if a heart attack is suspected.

MANAGING BLOOD PRESSURE IN A DISASTER SETTING

Patients who take regular blood pressure medication must not stop suddenly — sudden discontinuation can trigger a rebound hypertensive crisis.

If medications are lost or unavailable:
Reduce physical exertion and stay calm.
Reduce salt intake — avoid processed food and excessive salt added to food.
Drink adequate water.
Notify relief medical teams immediately so that medication supplies can be sourced.

Pakistan's NDMA and provincial disaster health teams deploy mobile medical units during major disasters. Inform them of any patient requiring regular medication for hypertension, diabetes, or epilepsy.
