Anaphylaxis — Severe Allergic Reaction Emergency

TOPIC: anaphylaxis, severe allergic reaction, allergy, allergic shock, bee sting allergy, throat swelling

RECOGNIZING ANAPHYLAXIS:
Anaphylaxis develops rapidly — usually within minutes of exposure to a trigger. Look for symptoms in two or more body systems at the same time:
- Skin: sudden hives (raised itchy welts), widespread redness and flushing, swelling of the face, lips, tongue, or throat
- Breathing: difficulty breathing, wheezing, noisy breathing, sensation of throat tightening, hoarse voice, stridor (high-pitched sound when inhaling)
- Circulation: rapid weak pulse, sudden dizziness, fainting or loss of consciousness
- Digestive: nausea, vomiting, severe abdominal cramps
- Neurological: sudden severe anxiety, confusion, loss of consciousness
Throat swelling is the most immediately life-threatening symptom — it can block the airway within minutes.

WHEN TO SEEK EMERGENCY CARE:
Anaphylaxis is always a medical emergency. Call Rescue 1122 or Edhi 115 immediately. State that the person is having a severe allergic reaction. All patients with suspected anaphylaxis must go to hospital even if they feel better — symptoms can return 8–72 hours later without re-exposure to the trigger.

WHAT TO DO WHILE WAITING:
If epinephrine (adrenaline) is NOT available (most common situation in the field):
1. Lay the person flat. If they are struggling to breathe, allow them to sit upright.
2. Remove the trigger if still present — scrape out an insect stinger, move away from the insect source.
3. Loosen tight clothing, especially around the neck.
4. Keep them calm and still. Do not give food or water.
5. Monitor breathing and pulse continuously.
6. If they lose consciousness and stop breathing, begin CPR immediately.

If epinephrine (adrenaline) IS available:
1. Inject into the outer thigh muscle — not in a vein, not in the buttock.
2. Adults: 0.3–0.5 mg intramuscular. Children: 0.01 mg per kg body weight.
3. A second dose can be given after 5–15 minutes if symptoms do not improve.
4. Hospital care is still required even after epinephrine — it buys time, it is not a cure.

DO NOT:
- Do not give antihistamines (chlorphenamine) as the primary treatment — they are too slow and may give false reassurance.
- Do not give aspirin or ibuprofen.
- Do not leave the person alone.
- Do not assume the reaction is over when symptoms improve — biphasic anaphylaxis means symptoms can return hours later.

ANAPHYLAXIS TRIGGERS IN PAKISTAN DISASTER SETTINGS:
- Insect stings: bee and wasp stings become more common when hives are disturbed by floods or rescue operations.
- Food allergies: relief food may contain allergens unfamiliar to the patient (nuts, dairy).
- Medications: penicillin, aspirin, NSAIDs administered during mass treatment campaigns.
- Snake venom: some venomous bites cause an anaphylactic response in addition to direct venom toxicity.

RECOGNIZING ANAPHYLAXIS IN CHILDREN:
Children may not be able to describe throat tightening. Look for: sudden excessive crying, refusal to eat, drooling, rapid breathing, sudden pallor, and slumping or limpness — in combination after a known exposure, these are warning signs.
