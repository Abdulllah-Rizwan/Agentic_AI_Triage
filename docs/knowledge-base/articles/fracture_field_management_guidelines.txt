Fractures — Recognition and Field Immobilization

Fractures (broken bones) are extremely common in disaster situations — from flood debris, building collapse, falls, and vehicle accidents. Proper field immobilization prevents further injury, reduces blood loss, reduces pain, and makes transport safer. Improperly managed fractures — especially femur (thigh) and pelvis fractures — can cause life-threatening internal bleeding.

RECOGNIZING A FRACTURE

Suspect a fracture when any of the following are present after an injury:
Pain at a specific point, worsening with movement or pressure.
Swelling and bruising around the injury.
Visible deformity — the limb looks bent, shortened, or out of alignment.
Inability to move the affected limb normally.
A crack or snap was heard at the time of injury.
The bone is visible through the skin (open/compound fracture).

CRITICAL FRACTURES — CALL FOR EMERGENCY HELP IMMEDIATELY

These fractures cause life-threatening internal bleeding:
Femur (thigh bone): A fractured femur can trap 1–2 litres of blood in the thigh. The patient will show signs of shock: pale, cold, sweating, rapid weak pulse.
Pelvis fracture: Can cause massive uncontrolled internal bleeding. Signs: pain in the hips and lower back, inability to walk, shock signs.
Spine (neck or back): Any fall from height, crushing injury, or high-speed trauma — assume spinal injury (see below).

GENERAL PRINCIPLES OF FRACTURE IMMOBILIZATION

Immobilize the fracture in the position found. Do not try to straighten or realign bones.
The splint must extend above and below the fracture — it must immobilize the joints on both sides of the break.
Pad splints with cloth, clothing, or foam to prevent pressure sores.
Secure splints with strips of cloth, bandages, or torn clothing — firm but not so tight as to cut off circulation.
After splinting, check circulation: can the patient feel their fingers or toes? Are they warm and a normal colour? If the limb goes cold, numb, or changes colour, the splint is too tight — loosen it.

ARM FRACTURE — SPLINTING AND SLING

Use any rigid material as a splint: a wooden board, thick cardboard, folded magazines, or bundled sticks.
Pad the splint and tie it along the length of the arm.
Make a sling: fold a large cloth into a triangle, place under the arm and tie around the neck. The elbow should be at 90 degrees.
The arm should rest across the chest.

LEG FRACTURE — SPLINTING

For lower leg (tibia/fibula): use two padded boards on either side of the leg, or tie the injured leg to the uninjured leg with cloth strips placed above and below the fracture.
For thigh (femur): requires a longer splint from armpit to foot, or traction splint if available. Keep the patient lying flat. Monitor for shock.
Do not allow the patient to walk on a fractured leg.

OPEN FRACTURE (BONE THROUGH SKIN)

Do not push the bone back in.
Do not probe or clean inside the wound.
Cover the wound with a clean, damp cloth to keep it moist and prevent drying.
Immobilize the limb in the position found.
Get to hospital urgently — open fractures become infected rapidly.

SUSPECTED SPINAL FRACTURE — DO NOT MOVE CARELESSLY

Suspect spinal injury after: falls from height, roof collapse, motor vehicle accident, diving into water, or any direct blow to the back or neck.

Signs suggesting spinal injury: pain in the neck or back, numbness or tingling in arms or legs, inability to move arms or legs, bladder or bowel loss of control after trauma.

If spinal injury is suspected:
Do not move the person unless they are in immediate danger (fire, rising water).
Hold the head still in the position found using both hands. Keep the head, neck, and spine in a line.
If they must be moved: log-roll technique — one person holds the head while at least 2–3 others roll the body as a single unit onto a rigid board (door, plank). Move as a team on a count.
Do not bend, twist, or flex the neck or spine under any circumstances.

JOINT DISLOCATIONS

A dislocation is when a joint is forced out of its normal position. It causes severe pain, swelling, and an abnormal shape at the joint.
Do not attempt to pop the joint back in — this requires medical expertise. Incorrect attempts can fracture bones, trap nerves, and cut off blood supply.
Immobilize in the position found, support with a sling or padding, and transport to hospital.
