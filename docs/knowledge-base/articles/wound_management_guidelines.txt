Wound and Cut Management — First Aid Guide

TOPIC: wound, cut, laceration, scrape, gash, deep cut, open wound, zakhm

RECOGNIZING WOUND SEVERITY:
Minor wound: small cut or scrape, bleeding controlled within 10 minutes of pressure, no deep tissue visible, edges come together naturally.

Moderate wound: cut longer than 2 cm or deep enough to see yellow fat tissue; jagged or gaping edges that will not stay closed; wound on the face, hand, or over a joint; wound from a rusty, dirty, or animal-contaminated object.

Severe wound:
- Bleeding that does not slow after 10 minutes of firm continuous pressure
- Blood spurting in pulses (arterial bleed)
- Wound to the chest, abdomen, neck, or groin
- Embedded object (knife, glass, metal) in the wound
- Bone visible through the wound
- Patient is pale, cold, confused, or losing consciousness from blood loss

Signs of infection (usually 24–72 hours after injury):
- Increasing redness spreading outward from the wound edges
- Wound is hot and swollen
- Pus or cloudy fluid leaking from the wound
- Foul smell
- Red streaks running up the arm or leg from the wound
- Fever or chills

WHEN TO SEEK EMERGENCY CARE:
Go to hospital immediately for:
- Any severe wound listed above
- Wounds with embedded objects — do not remove them
- Wounds to the chest, abdomen, neck, or groin
- Wound from a rusty or heavily contaminated object — tetanus injection may be needed
- Signs of infection with fever, spreading redness, or red streaks
- Any wound that has not stopped bleeding after 10–15 minutes of firm pressure
- Animal or human bites (see separate article)

WHAT TO DO WHILE WAITING:
For minor cuts and scrapes:
1. Wash your hands before touching the wound.
2. Apply gentle firm pressure with a clean cloth or dressing to stop bleeding. Hold for at least 5–10 minutes without releasing to check.
3. Rinse the wound under clean running water for several minutes to remove dirt and debris. Do not use hydrogen peroxide or iodine — these damage healing tissue.
4. Wash around the wound with soap and water. Keep soap out of the wound itself.
5. Remove any visible dirt or debris using clean tweezers. If debris is deeply embedded, do not probe — go to hospital.
6. Pat dry with a clean cloth and cover with a clean bandage or dressing.
7. Change the dressing at least once daily or when it becomes wet or dirty.
8. Give paracetamol for pain.

For moderate to severe wounds (while getting to hospital):
1. Apply firm continuous pressure with the cleanest cloth available. Do not remove the cloth if it soaks through — add more on top.
2. Raise the injured limb above heart level if possible while maintaining pressure.
3. For embedded objects: do NOT pull them out. Pad around the object with cloth and apply pressure beside it. The object may be plugging a blood vessel.
4. Keep the patient lying down and warm.

DO NOT:
- Do not remove embedded objects from wounds.
- Do not apply toothpaste, butter, haldi (turmeric paste), or any traditional remedies — these introduce bacteria and complicate hospital treatment.
- Do not close a contaminated wound with tape or stitches before it has been properly cleaned.
- Do not check a pressure dressing by lifting it every few minutes — this breaks the clot. Press and hold.
- Do not use hydrogen peroxide or iodine on the wound — they damage tissue and slow healing.

PREVENTION:
- Wear shoes or sandals in all disaster-affected areas — flood debris, broken glass, and metal are common underfoot hazards.
- Cover all existing wounds and cuts with waterproof dressings before walking in floodwater — floodwater contains sewage bacteria that rapidly infect open wounds.
- Ensure tetanus vaccination is up to date — any wound from soil, rust, or animal contact carries tetanus risk if not vaccinated within the last 5–10 years.
